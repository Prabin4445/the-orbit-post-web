    Bananas Are Berries. Strawberries Are Not.

    Is it a myth? Botanists say no. A berry must grow from one flower with one ovary and hold its seeds inside three fleshy layers. The banana passes that test. The strawberry, an aggregate accessory fruit whose red flesh is a swollen flower base, does not.

    Key points

    • Here is the answer, up front: yes, it is a myth, at least the way most people mean it.
    • A true berry has three fleshy layers.

    Here is the answer, up front: yes, it is a myth, at least the way most people mean it. Botanists say the banana is a berry and the strawberry is not. The word berry means one thing in a kitchen and another thing in a plant lab, and the plant lab definition is strict: a berry is a fleshy fruit that grows from a single flower with one ovary, with the seeds held inside the flesh.

    The three layer test

    A true berry has three fleshy layers. The outer skin is the exocarp. The middle flesh is the mesocarp. The inner part that holds the seeds is the endocarp. A berry also needs two or more seeds, and it must grow from a flower with a single ovary. The banana passes every part of that test. Its peel is the skin, its soft flesh is the middle, and its seeds sit inside the fruit. In the bananas sold in shops the seeds are tiny and undeveloped, the small dark specks in the center. In wild bananas the seeds are large and obvious.

    Advertisement

    The banana is not a lonely odd case. By the same definition, tomatoes, eggplants, kiwis and peppers are berries too. Oranges count as a special kind of berry called a hesperidium, with a rind and juicy segments inside. Grapes, the fruit most people would name first, are true berries as well.

    So what is a strawberry?

    A strawberry fails the test at the first step. It grows from a single flower, but that flower carries many ovaries, not one. The red part you eat is not the wall of an ovary at all. It is the swollen receptacle, the base of the flower that held the ovaries up. Botanists call the strawberry an aggregate accessory fruit: aggregate because many ovaries took part, accessory because the tasty part came from tissue outside the ovaries.

    And the seeds are not seeds. The small pale specks on the surface are achenes, and each achene is a tiny dry fruit with a single seed inside it. One strawberry is, in botanical terms, a crowd of small fruits riding on a sweet red base that is not a fruit wall.

    Raspberries fail the berry test too, for a related reason. They also form from many ovaries in one flower. The names simply settled long before the science did.

    Why the two vocabularies split

    Everyday language sorts fruit by size, sweetness and habit: small, soft and eaten by the handful sounds like a berry. Botany sorts fruit by construction: which flower part built it, and where the seeds sit. Neither system is wrong. They answer different questions. But only one of them is a scientific category, and under that category the banana on your counter is a berry and the strawberry in your fridge is not.

    Keep the fact in your pocket for the next grocery run. The produce aisle is full of quiet rebels: berries wearing the wrong names, and impostors wearing the right ones.
