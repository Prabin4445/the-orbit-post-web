    Crypto Flatlines as Stocks Waver and Trump Bets on Russian Diesel

    Bitcoin barely moved overnight while Wall Street opened Monday split in two directions. The money story that matters: Brussels hit Russia with its biggest-ever sanctions package as Washington buys Russian diesel to cut pump prices before the midterms.

    OPBy Orbit PostOctober 12, 2026 · 14:30 UTC5 min read
    Share
    Read inENESNEHIFR

    Key points

    • Bitcoin spent the night going nowhere.
    • Nothing in crypto earned a second look overnight.
    • Monday's live quotes told two stories at once.

    Bitcoin spent the night going nowhere. Wall Street opened Monday with industrial stocks up and tech stocks down. And the money story that matters most this morning is a tug of war over energy prices: Brussels slapped its biggest-ever sanctions package on Russia's weapons industry while Washington buys Russian diesel to push pump prices down before the November midterms.

    Crypto: flat, and that is the honest headline

    Nothing in crypto earned a second look overnight. Our desk's snapshots show bitcoin at $82,885, down 0.11 percent from roughly 24 hours earlier. Ether climbed 1.23 percent to $2,530.74, the only move worth mentioning. Solana added 0.28 percent to $109.62, XRP slipped 0.71 percent to $1.39, and BNB eased 0.17 percent to $747.06. That is as close to flat as markets get: none of our wire alert thresholds tripped, and the desk published zero flash articles overnight.

    A split open on Wall Street

    Monday's live quotes told two stories at once. As of about 10:30 a.m. Eastern time, the Dow-tracking DIA was up 0.41 percent at $518.25 while the Nasdaq-heavy QQQ fell 0.72 percent to $745.83. The S&P 500 ETF, SPY, sat nearly still at $777.80, down 0.10 percent. Old-economy stocks are holding up; the AI trade is taking a breath. Friday's close showed the same split forming: SPY at $778.57, QQQ at $751.27, DIA at $516.11.

    Among the big seven, Meta stood out. It jumped 3.22 percent to $741.84, the only major tech stock to move 3 percent or more in either direction. Nvidia was the weakest, down 1.75 percent to $225.26. Apple rose 1.40 percent to $341.36, bouncing back after a 1.11 percent slide Friday that left it at $336.64. Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Tesla all stayed within a percent of unchanged.

    The energy tug of war

    Brussels moved first this morning. EU foreign ministers formally adopted nearly 1,600 new sanctions listings aimed at Russia's military-industrial complex, the largest single package the bloc has ever agreed. The EU's own announcement counts 1,569 listings: 826 companies and 743 individuals, mostly chief executives. The package zeroes in on mid-level suppliers, the firms that make the parts for missiles, drones and aircraft, and over half connect to the supply chain for ballistic missiles. "We are making it significantly harder, slower and more expensive for Russia to produce the weapons it uses to kill Ukrainians," said Kaja Kallas, the EU's foreign policy chief.

    Washington is pulling the other way. On Friday, President Trump announced a deal with Vladimir Putin for Russia to ship 300,000 tons of diesel right away, 500,000 more in November, another million tons after that, and a further 3 million tons "within a short period of time." The Treasury Department followed with a temporary general license letting Russian diesel flow.

    The numbers explain the urgency. U.S. diesel hit a record $6.528 a gallon on September 22, according to AAA, and midterm elections are three weeks away on November 3. But analysts doubt the deal moves the needle: the total is roughly 36 million barrels, and Rebecca Babin of CIBC Private Wealth told MarketWatch the deliveries may not fully materialize and could arrive "too little, too late."

    Meanwhile the backdrop keeps getting messier. Trump has been pushing an "energy ceasefire" between Russia and Ukraine that neither side has confirmed, and on Saturday he warned Ukraine's president to stop striking Russian refineries, blaming those attacks for diesel prices. Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelenskyy pushed back, saying the Middle East war is the bigger factor. The weekend backed that up in a grim way: Houthi missile strikes hit Riyadh's King Khalid airport on Saturday, killing 13 people, and struck again on Sunday.

    Oil is watching all of this at once: Brent traded at $104 a barrel Monday morning and WTI at about $91, both slightly lower, as traders bet more Russian diesel might loosen a tight market. ING's commodity strategists warned the deal "is not going to solve the tightness in the middle distillate market."

    Our October 9 report covered the tanker attacks that pushed Brent to $104 and our October 10 report covered the diesel deal itself. This morning's story is what came next.

    What it means for your money

    For most people's savings, this is a steady-as-she-goes morning. If your 401(k) sits in index funds, the S&P 500 and the Dow are hovering near record territory while tech stocks pause. That split is a reminder the market's gains lean on a handful of AI names: when Nvidia drops 1.75 percent before lunch, your portfolio feels it.

    Diesel matters more than bitcoin for the typical household budget. Record diesel prices feed straight into the cost of moving goods, and freight costs land in grocery prices and everything else. If Trump's Russian diesel deal actually delivers, it could ease inflation, giving the Federal Reserve room to keep cutting interest rates. Rate cuts are what bring mortgage rates down. If the deal fizzles, expect the Fed to stay cautious and mortgages to stay stuck where they are.

    What to watch next

    Three dates matter. Apple reports earnings on November 2, and investors will listen for its AI spending plans. The midterms follow on November 3, which is why diesel prices are suddenly a White House priority. And watch whether any of that promised Russian diesel actually loads onto ships: announcements move headlines, only barrels move prices.
