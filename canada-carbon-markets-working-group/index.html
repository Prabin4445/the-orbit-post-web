    Canada Launches Working Group to Link Its Patchwork Carbon Markets

    Federal, provincial and territorial officials held a first meeting this week on linking Canada's separate carbon markets, a move business groups say could cut the cost of cutting emissions.

    • Canada's governments have started the work of stitching their separate carbon markets into something closer to one system.
    • First Ministers agreed in July 2026 to create the group.

    Canada's governments have started the work of stitching their separate carbon markets into something closer to one system. A new federal, provincial and territorial working group on carbon markets held its first meeting this week and discussed a draft workplan, the Government of Canada said in a statement on Friday, October 23.

    First Ministers agreed in July 2026 to create the group. It brings together representatives from every province and territory plus the federal government. Its job is to explore different models for linking carbon markets across the country, so that industrial emitters face clearer and more consistent rules when they operate in more than one province.

    Advertisement

    Why it matters

    Canada does not have one carbon market. It has a patchwork. Large industrial emitters pay for pollution under systems that differ by province, and a credit earned in one system does not always count in another. Business groups have complained for years that the patchwork raises costs, slows investment and makes it hard to plan big projects such as carbon capture plants, clean steel and low carbon fuel facilities.

    The federal statement says well functioning carbon markets can unlock new investment in clean industrial growth and cut the emissions that cause climate change. It also frames the work as part of a wider push to build one Canadian economy and remove internal trade barriers between provinces. Linking markets is one of the few climate files where Ottawa and the provinces can gain something at the same time: Ottawa gets a more coherent national system, and provinces keep control of their own systems while gaining access to a bigger pool of buyers and sellers.

    Market analysts have made the same argument. A September 2026 vision paper from the International Emissions Trading Association's Canada program said moving from fragmented markets toward interoperability and linkage would expand supply and demand for compliance credits, improve liquidity and reduce uneven compliance burdens, while preserving provincial flexibility in system design. The paper pointed to the new working group as the immediate policy lever and to the 2026 federal benchmark process as the moment to set common conditions.

    The context

    Industrial carbon pricing is the part of Canada's climate plan that survived the political fights over consumer fuel charges. It covers the biggest emitters in sectors such as oil and gas, steel, cement and chemicals. How tight those systems are, and how freely credits trade between them, decides real money: the cost of a tonne of emissions for a plant in Alberta can differ from the cost faced by a similar plant in Ontario or Quebec.

    Linking is not a new idea. Quebec has run a linked cap and trade market with California for years. What is new is a table where every province and territory sits with Ottawa to look at linking models for the industrial systems that now sit apart.

    What happens next

    The group has a draft workplan, not a deal. The questions ahead are technical and political at once: which credits each system will recognize, how to keep prices from diverging so far that linking breaks down, and how common rules can be written without taking away provincial control. The federal benchmark process running through 2026 is the next fixed point. Industry will watch for signals on scarcity and long term price direction, because a linked market with clear rules is easier to invest against than thirteen separate ones.

    For ordinary Canadians, the effect is indirect but real. Carbon costs sit inside the price of cement, steel, fertilizer and fuel. A market that works across provincial lines should cut the cost of meeting the same emissions targets, since reductions can happen where they are cheapest. Whether the working group delivers that depends on the models it studies this fall and the choices First Ministers make when it reports back.

    • Working group agreed by First Ministers in July 2026
    • First meeting held this week, with a draft workplan discussed
    • Goal: study models for linking carbon markets across Canada
    • Every province and territory sits at the table with Ottawa
