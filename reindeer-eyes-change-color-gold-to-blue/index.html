    Reindeer Eyes Turn Gold in Summer and Deep Blue in Winter

    The reflective layer behind a reindeer retina shifts from gold to blue as winter pressure packs its fibres tighter, making the eye far more sensitive in Arctic twilight.

    OPBy Orbit PostOctober 23, 2026 · 16:20 UTC2 min read
    Share

    Key points

    • A reindeer does not keep the same eye colour all year.
    • The change sounds like a trick of light.
    • Arctic reindeer live above the Arctic Circle.

    A reindeer does not keep the same eye colour all year. In summer, the reflective layer inside its eye shines gold. In winter, that same layer turns deep blue.

    The change sounds like a trick of light. It is real anatomy. It helps the animal see through months of Arctic dark.

    Life Between Endless Light and Endless Dark

    Arctic reindeer live above the Arctic Circle. Summer brings weeks of continuous daylight. Winter brings weeks of near darkness, with long stretches of twilight in place of a normal day.

    That swing is hard on eyes. An eye built for bright summer days must also work when almost no light reaches the ground. Reindeer solve the problem by changing the eye itself with the season.

    The Mirror Behind the Retina

    Like many mammals, reindeer have a tapetum lucidum. It is a reflective layer behind the retina. It bounces light back through the eye and gives photoreceptors a second chance to catch it.

    The layer also causes eyeshine. When light hits an animal eye at night, the tapetum reflects some of it back out. In reindeer, the colour of that reflection changes.

    A 2013 study in Proceedings of the Royal Society B examined the seasonal shift. Researchers at University College London and the University of Tromsoe in Norway led the work. Lead researcher Professor Glen Jeffery said it was the first time a colour change of this kind had been shown in mammals.

    How Pressure Turns Gold to Blue

    In winter, reindeer pupils stay dilated for months. The animals keep their eyes wide open to catch scarce light. That raises pressure inside the eye.

    Higher pressure squeezes the tapetum. The collagen fibres inside it pack closer together. The tighter spacing shifts the reflected light toward blue. It also scatters more light across the photoreceptors.

    Reports of the study put the gain at between 100 and 1,000 times more light sensitive in winter. The trade off is clear. A scattered image is less sharp. The winter eye trades crisp detail for the ability to detect faint light and movement.

    Why Blue Fits the Arctic Winter

    Arctic twilight is rich in blue light. A blue reflecting eye matches the light that is actually available during the long winter murk.

    The shift also helps with contrast. Lichen and wolf fur reflect little blue. Against snow, food and predators stand out dark. A reindeer can spot a moving predator sooner. It can also find forage that would vanish in flat grey light.

    In summer the process reverses. Pupils contract more often, pressure falls, the fibres spread out, and the tapetum reflects gold again. The eye gives up extreme sensitivity for a sharper view in bright conditions.

    The result is not two different animals. It is one animal with an eye that follows the season, gold for the bright months and blue for the dark ones.
