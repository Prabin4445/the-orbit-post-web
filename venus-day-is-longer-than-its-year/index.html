    A Day on Venus Is Longer Than a Year on Venus

    Venus takes 243 Earth days to spin once and only 225 Earth days to circle the Sun, according to NASA. It also spins backward, so the Sun would rise in the west, and a sunrise comes only once every 117 Earth days.

    Share
    Read inENESNEHIFR

    Key points

    • A day on Venus lasts longer than a year on Venus.
    • The 243 day figure is one full rotation, measured against the distant stars.
    • There are two clocks, and Venus pulls them apart.

    A day on Venus lasts longer than a year on Venus. NASA puts the numbers plainly: Venus takes 243 Earth days to spin once on its axis, and only 225 Earth days to travel all the way around the Sun. The planet finishes a full lap of the Sun before it finishes a single turn. No other planet in our solar system runs its clock this way.

    Two different days, both real

    The 243 day figure is one full rotation, measured against the distant stars. But a day as people live it is the wait from one sunrise to the next, and on Venus that wait is different. NASA Space Place puts it this way: on Venus the Sun rises every 117 Earth days. The gap between one sunrise and the next is about 117 Earth days, even though a single full spin takes 243.

    Advertisement

    There are two clocks, and Venus pulls them apart. The spin clock says 243 days for a single turn. The Sun clock says the Sun comes back to the same place in the sky after about 117 Earth days. The two clocks disagree because Venus is orbiting while it spins, and because it spins backward. On Earth the two clocks differ by only about four minutes. On Venus they differ by more than four months.

    The Sun rises in the west

    Venus spins in the opposite direction to Earth and most other planets, a motion astronomers call retrograde. If you could stand on the surface, the Sun would rise in the west and set in the east. NASA Space Place gives the same figures for younger readers: about 243 Earth days to spin once, 225 Earth days for one trip around the Sun, and a sunrise every 117 Earth days.

    You could not stand there for long. Venus is the hottest planet in the solar system. Its thick atmosphere is rich in carbon dioxide, its clouds carry sulfuric acid, and the heat trapped at the surface is enough to melt lead. NASA's StarChild pages describe daytime temperatures reaching about 484 degrees Celsius. The slow spin also means there is no quick daily cool down. With a tilt of only about three degrees, Venus gets no real seasons either. The heat just stays.

    Why a backward, crawling spin matters

    Venus is close to Earth in size and mass, which is why scientists call it Earth's twin. The spin makes it a stranger instead. A planet that turns this slowly and in reverse forces scientists to rethink how planets settle into their rotations over billions of years, and what a thick atmosphere can do to a world over time.

    It also breaks the most basic habit in human timekeeping. On Earth, a year holds hundreds of days. On Venus, a year holds less than one full spin and about two sunrises. Birthdays would come faster than sunsets. The calendar says one thing. The sky says another. On Venus, the sky wins.
