    Why Onions Make Your Eyes Water, in One Chemical Chain

    A whole onion does nothing to your eyes. One cut changes everything. Here is how sulfur from soil becomes a gas that rises to your cornea and orders your brain to cry.

    OPBy Orbit PostOctober 16, 2026 · 17:19 UTC3 min read
    Share
    Read inENESNEHIFR

    Key points

    • A whole onion sitting on the counter does nothing to your eyes.
    • That switch is not magic.
    • An onion pulls sulfur from the soil as it grows.

    A whole onion sitting on the counter does nothing to your eyes. One knife cut later, you are blinking and wiping tears away.

    That switch is not magic. It is a chemical chain the onion keeps locked up until you break it open.

    Advertisement

    The parts stay separate

    An onion pulls sulfur from the soil as it grows. It stores that sulfur in compounds inside its cells.

    Inside those cells, the key ingredients sit apart. One set is sulfur compounds built from amino acid sulfoxides. Another is a group of enzymes called alliinases. While the onion stays whole, they never meet.

    That separation is the point. Nothing happens until the wall between them breaks.

    The chain when you cut

    Cutting breaks the cells. The parts mix for the first time.

    First, the alliinases act on the amino acid sulfoxides. That reaction produces sulfenic acid.

    Next, a second enzyme steps in. It is called lachrymatory factor synthase. It turns the sulfenic acid into a volatile gas named syn-propanethial-S-oxide. Scientists call that gas the lachrymatory factor.

    Volatile means the gas escapes fast. It rises off the board toward your face. Robert H. Rosa Jr., an expert at Texas A&M College of Medicine, described the whole sequence as a complicated chemical process, according to ScienceDaily. You never see the gas. You feel what it does next.

    Why your eyes flood

    The gas reaches your eyes and settles on the surface. It irritates nerves in the cornea, the clear front layer of the eye.

    Those nerves send an alarm to the brain. The brain gives a simple order: flush the irritant out. It signals the tear glands to release tears.

    The tears are not sadness. They are a rinse. Your body tries to wash the chemical off the eye. Cut more onion, and more gas rises, so the tears keep coming until the gas clears.

    A defense, not an accident

    This system did not evolve to bother cooks. It is a plant defense.

    The onion lives underground, where pests and animals can chew at it unseen. A bite triggers the same chain as a knife. The sharp gas pushes the attacker away. Your kitchen tears are that defense working exactly as built.

    Why some onions hit harder

    Not every onion brings the same tears. White, yellow and red onions tend to produce more tears than sweet onions. Green onions and scallions also tend to be milder.

    The difference is supply. The stronger types hold more of the enzymes and sulfur compounds the chain needs. The milder types make less gas, so your eyes get a smaller dose.

    How to cut with fewer tears

    You cannot stop the chain, but you can slow it or steer it away from your face.

    Chill the onion first. Cold slows the reactions, so less gas forms at once. Use a sharp knife. A sharp blade slides through cells instead of crushing them, so fewer cells break. Add ventilation. A fan or an open window moves the gas away before it reaches your eyes. None of this changes the chemistry. Each trick just lowers the gas that reaches your cornea.

    The next time your eyes water over a board, you will know the route. Soil sulfur, stored compounds, two enzymes, one rising gas, and a brain that would rather cry than sit still.
