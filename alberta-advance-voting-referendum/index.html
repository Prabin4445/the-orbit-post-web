    Advance Voting Opens in Alberta Vote With Separation on the Ballot

    Advance polls run Oct. 13 to Oct. 17 in Alberta's ten question referendum. The tenth question asks whether Alberta stays in Canada or starts the legal process toward a binding separation vote. Leger polling puts remain at 70 percent.

    EDMONTON: Advance voting opened across Alberta on Tuesday, Oct. 13, in a provincial referendum that asks voters ten questions at once, ending with the biggest one in Canadian politics: should Alberta remain a province of Canada, or should its government start the legal process toward a binding vote on separation?

    Advance polls run from Oct. 13 to Oct. 17, from 9 a.m. to 8 p.m., and voters can cast ballots at any advance voting place inside their own electoral district. Referendum day is Monday, Oct. 19, when polls run the same hours but voters must use the voting place assigned to their home address. Because there are ten questions, each on its own ballot, the hand count starts when polls close on Oct. 19 and the unofficial count must finish within 48 hours, by 8 p.m. on Oct. 21.

    Advertisement

    Why it matters

    The Oct. 19 vote does not take Alberta out of Canada. Elections Alberta describes the separation question as non-binding. A majority for the second option would direct the provincial government to begin the legal process under the Canadian Constitution toward a later, binding referendum on separation. The other nine questions are not small. Five deal with immigration and access to provincial programs, including citizenship proof for voting. Four deal with constitutional change, including Senate abolition and Alberta law taking priority in some areas. A voter can back more provincial power on some questions and still vote to remain in Canada on the tenth.

    What makes this week heavy is the company Alberta keeps. Quebec recently elected a party promising an independence referendum in its first term. Two provinces testing the exit at the same time puts Ottawa in a position it has not faced before, arguing for the country on two fronts at once.

    The context

    Premier Danielle Smith has said she will vote to remain, and that the threshold on the separation question is 50 percent plus one. Asked whether she would hold another separation vote if Albertans choose to stay, Smith said she would not, not as long as she is premier, in an Oct. 9 CBC interview. Leaders of the independence movement have said the campaign continues whatever the count, arguing that the grievances behind it, over equalization, energy policy and Ottawa's treatment of the province, will still be there on Oct. 20.

    The polling says the remain side starts well ahead. A Leger survey of 1,001 Alberta adults, taken Oct. 2 to Oct. 4 and published Oct. 8, found 70 percent for remaining in Canada and 22 percent for starting the process toward a binding separation referendum. A Janet Brown survey for CBC pointed the same way, at 72 percent and 23 percent. Polls are snapshots, not results, and referendum campaigns can move late. Turnout is the open question. Elections Alberta had processed just over 658,000 special ballot applications by Oct. 1, approving and sending more than 652,000 of them, a sign of unusual early interest. Applications for mail ballots closed on Sept. 25, and completed mail ballots must reach Elections Alberta by 5 p.m. on Oct. 16.

    Prime Minister Mark Carney used his Oct. 12 Thanksgiving message to argue for unity, speaking about his own Alberta upbringing and warning that division would weaken national projects.

    What happens next

    Advance voting is the first real test of turnout. If advance numbers run high through Oct. 17, both camps will claim momentum, but the count that matters starts at 8 p.m. on Oct. 19, with the separation question counted first. A clear remain result would likely push the fight back into normal federal-provincial bargaining, where Smith has said she wants a stronger Alberta inside Canada. A narrow remain result would keep the movement alive with proof it can compete. A vote to start the separation process would open a legal and constitutional fight that would run for years, through Ottawa, the courts and the other provinces.

    Practical notes for voters: bring identification that proves name and address. Elections Alberta accepts more than 50 forms of ID. Vouching, which was allowed in earlier elections, is no longer accepted. Students who normally live in Alberta but study elsewhere can still vote as Alberta residents.

    Key numbers

    Referendum day: Oct. 19, 2026, polls 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. Mountain Time. Advance voting: Oct. 13 to Oct. 17. Questions: 10, each on its own ballot. Leger poll: 70 percent remain, 22 percent start the separation process, from 1,001 adults polled Oct. 2 to Oct. 4. Mail ballots processed by Oct. 1: just over 658,000 applications, more than 652,000 sent out. Mail ballot deadline: 5 p.m. on Oct. 16. Unofficial count deadline: 8 p.m. on Oct. 21.
