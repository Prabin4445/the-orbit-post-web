    Canada-India Trade Deal: What $70 Billion by 2030 Really Takes

    Canada and India want to more than double two-way trade to $70 billion a year by 2030 and finish a trade deal by the end of 2026. The fifth round of talks in Ottawa shows real momentum, and the hard parts are now clear.

    OPBy Orbit PostOctober 14, 2026 · 14:21 UTC4 min read
    Share

    Key points

    • Canada and India have set a large target: $70 billion in annual two-way trade by 2030, more than double the $30.4 billion in goods and services exchanged in 2025.
    • The fourth round ended in New Delhi on September 18, weeks before Ottawa opened.
    • The traffic around the talks keeps growing.

    Canada and India have set a large target: $70 billion in annual two-way trade by 2030, more than double the $30.4 billion in goods and services exchanged in 2025. The tool is a trade deal, the Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement, or CEPA. The fifth round of talks ran in Ottawa from October 5 to 9, 2026, and both sides aim to conclude by the end of 2026.

    The fourth round ended in New Delhi on September 18, weeks before Ottawa opened. The talks were relaunched during Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney's visit to India in March 2026. Trade ministers Maninder Sidhu and Piyush Goyal have met nine times since May 2025, including at the G20 Trade Ministers Meeting in Milwaukee.

    Advertisement

    The traffic around the talks keeps growing. The Team Canada Trade Mission runs October 12 to 17 in Mumbai and Bengaluru with nearly 300 delegates, Canada's largest to India in over 30 years. Its sectors list what both economies want next: energy, critical minerals, aerospace, artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, clean technology, life sciences, agri-food and forestry. Foreign Minister Anita Anand is in New Delhi from October 13 to 16 and met External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar. The Orbit Post reported the mission as news on October 12.

    The numbers behind the target

    Merchandise trade in 2025 was $13.6 billion, in India's favor. Canadian exports to India were $3.9 billion, led by vegetables, mineral fuels and oils, and wood pulp. Imports from India were $9.7 billion, led by precious stones and metals, machinery and pharmaceuticals. Canada sells India food, fuel and raw materials. India sells Canada higher value manufactured goods and medicine.

    Services are the quiet giant here. Canadian services exports were $15.2 billion in 2025, several times all Canadian goods exports to India, with education-related travel the largest share.

    Key numbers

    Target: $70 billion by 2030. Trade in 2025: $30.4 billion in goods and services. Merchandise trade: $13.6 billion. Canadian exports to India: $3.9 billion. Imports from India: $9.7 billion. Services exports: $15.2 billion in 2025. Mission: nearly 300 delegates, October 12 to 17, Mumbai and Bengaluru. Sidhu and Goyal: nine meetings since May 2025.

    Why Canada wants this deal

    Canada's stated motive is to diversify exports away from heavy dependence on the United States, under its Indo-Pacific Strategy. A CEPA with India gives that strategy a working part instead of a slogan. India offers scale, a growing market for Canadian energy, minerals and food, and demand for Canadian services and technology. For India, the deal means steady access to energy, critical minerals for factories and clean technology plans, and farm inputs, plus Canadian markets for Indian goods, pharmaceuticals and services. Each side gains something it cannot easily get from the other today.

    What a deal would change

    For farmers, the change is certainty. Canada wants predictable, enforceable access for pulses and farm inputs, because India has changed pulse import duties several times. A farmer deciding what to plant, or a buyer signing a contract, needs to know the duty at the border will not change mid-season. Written, enforceable rules are worth money even before any duty falls.

    For students, services are the heart of it. Education-related travel is already the largest share of Canadian services exports to India, and worker visas are on the table in these talks. Visa and recognition rules decide whether the flow grows or stalls, and the outcome will touch students, workers, and the colleges and firms that depend on them.

    For energy firms and miners, the mission's sector list is the signal: energy and critical minerals top the Team Canada agenda. A CEPA that locks in access and clear rules makes long contracts easier to sign, and long contracts are how energy and mineral projects get financed.

    For shoppers, the effects would arrive slowly: a wider range of goods, from pharmaceuticals to machinery to food, moving between the two countries under clearer and possibly lower duties.

    The hard issues

    Three problems stand out. First, pulses and farm inputs: Canada wants access it can enforce, and India has changed pulse import duties several times, exactly the habit a trade deal exists to stop. Second, technical standards: goods that pass inspection in one country can stall at the other border if standards and testing do not line up. Third, worker visas: services trade runs on people moving, and visa rules can hold back everything from students to specialists.

    What could still break it, and what happens next

    The deal could break on the same three issues. If India will not give enforceable terms on pulses, Canadian farm support weakens. If standards talks stall, exporters get a deal on paper that does not move goods. If visa questions turn political in either country, the services trade that carries the relationship could get harder, not easier.

    The near calendar helps. The mission runs to October 17, Minister Anand is in New Delhi to October 16, and both sides aim to conclude by the end of 2026. Turning it into trade will take steady, repeated work, which Ottawa showed in good supply, and which the hard issues will keep testing.
