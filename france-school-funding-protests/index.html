    Tear Gas in France as 266,000 March for School Funding

    What began at one Paris-region high school has grown into the largest protest wave in months, pressuring the administration of outgoing President Macron.

    Riot police fired tear gas on crowds in Paris and other French cities on Tuesday as more than a quarter-million people took to the streets in support of high school students demanding more education funding.

    France's Interior Ministry estimated that 266,000 people demonstrated nationwide on Oct. 6, including 56,000 in Paris, according to the Associated Press. Nearly 500 people were detained during the day's marches. Large rallies also took place in Marseille, Strasbourg, Lille and other cities, making it the largest protest wave France has seen in months.

    Advertisement

    The clashes marked a sharp escalation for a movement led largely by teenagers. What began at a Paris-region high school in mid-September, over crumbling buildings, overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages and unequal conditions between schools, has grown into a nationwide wave of student-led protests putting direct pressure on the administration of outgoing President Emmanuel Macron.

    A youth-led movement

    Adult demonstrators turned out in significant numbers, and many described the students as the moral center of the protests. Léa Monimart, an adult marcher in Lille, told the AP why she was there:

    "We need to listen to them. They are 17 years old; they are not completely clueless, and they know what they are talking about."

    The students' demands are concrete: money to repair decaying school buildings, more teachers to ease overcrowding, and fairer conditions across districts. The movement has spread through the school system with remarkable speed, drawing support from parents, teachers and unions who see the students' grievances as symptoms of a broader funding crisis.

    The official response

    The government's response so far has combined promises of attention with a forceful police presence. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said the police response had been proportionate "in almost all cases," a formulation that did little to calm criticism of tear gas being used on crowds of teenagers. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said the government must listen to students' concerns, stopping short of announcing new funding.

    The tension between those two messages, listening to students while firing tear gas at their demonstrations, has become the central political fact of the crisis. Opposition figures and commentators have seized on the images of police confronting 17-year-olds, framing the administration's handling of the protests as a symbol of its broader relationship with young France.

    What comes next

    The administration of the outgoing president now faces a familiar French dilemma: a protest movement with broad public sympathy and specific demands, against a government in its final stretch with limited political capital. The students have shown no sign of standing down, and the adults marching beside them have given the movement a staying power that pure student protests often lack.

    Whether the government moves from listening to funding will determine whether Tuesday's quarter-million marchers were a peak or a beginning. For now, the tear gas in Paris has turned a school-funding dispute into a national political crisis.
