    Homecoming King Hands His Crown to His Classmate With Down Syndrome

    Senior Keny Cedanio Ramirez was crowned homecoming king at Mahanoy Area on Oct 16, then placed his sash and crown on finalist Axton Kuhla as about 1,100 students cheered.

    OPBy Orbit PostOctober 21, 2026 · 14:30 UTC4 min read
    Share
    Read inENESNEHIFR

    Key points

    • The gym was already loud when the name was read.
    • Ramirez was a finalist alongside Axton Kuhla, a classmate with Down syndrome.
    • The moment took seconds.

    The gym was already loud when the name was read. At Mahanoy Area Junior-Senior High School in Schuylkill County, Pennsylvania, senior Keny Cedanio Ramirez was announced as homecoming king on Friday, Oct 16, 2026. It was a districtwide homecoming pep rally. About 1,100 students filled the gym, from kindergartners to seniors.

    Ramirez was a finalist alongside Axton Kuhla, a classmate with Down syndrome. Ramirez received the sash and crown. Then he saw Kuhla tear up. Ramirez walked over to him and placed the sash and crown on him. The crowd cheered.

    Advertisement

    The moment took seconds. Its reach went far beyond the gym. Video taken by Dawn Bowers spread widely on social media. People who had never heard of Mahanoy watched a senior hand his own crown to a friend, in front of the whole school, without a speech and without waiting to be asked.

    What Ramirez said afterward

    Ramirez spoke to 6ABC, in remarks reported by Parade. He did not dress up the moment. He said he had not expected the result at all.

    "When I got crowned, I was honestly surprised. I did not think I was gonna win."

    He explained the choice in plain terms.

    "I just did what any friend would have done."

    Those two lines explain why the video kept moving. Ramirez did not present the act as a grand gesture. He treated it as friendship, the kind of thing a person does in the moment because the person next to him matters more than a title.

    He also gave a personal reason. He said he has a brother with autism, and he would hope someone would show his brother the same kindness. That hope shaped what he did when he saw Kuhla react. He credited his mother with teaching him the values behind the choice. The values were not abstract in that gym. They turned into a sash and a crown moved from one senior to another.

    A day Kuhla had waited for

    For Kuhla, homecoming was not a small date on a school calendar. His mother, Alyssa Shellhamer, told TODAY, in reporting on the story, that her son had looked forward to being a senior and to homecoming for years. He had waited for this stage of school life and for this week in particular.

    Shellhamer said he had been anxious before the rally. That anxiety stood out because it is unlike him. She described a son who is outgoing and who loves to sing and dance. A boy who loves a crowd felt the weight of one that day, because the day meant so much to him.

    That context makes the scene in the gym clearer. Kuhla did not just receive a crown. He received it from the student who had just won it, in front of about 1,100 schoolmates who all saw the handover and cheered it. The finalist who did not get the title from the announcement got it from a friend, by hand, in public view.

    Why the video spread

    School pep rallies happen every fall across the country. Most stay inside the building. This one left the building because the video showed a complete story in a few seconds. A winner was named. A friend was upset. The winner acted at once. No adult directed the scene, and no one had to explain it.

    Viewers also responded to how Ramirez talked about it later. He gave credit to his mother. He talked about his brother. He kept his own role small. That tone matched the act itself. People share moments that feel unplanned, and this one, by every account in the reporting, was decided on the spot when Ramirez saw Kuhla tear up.

    What the moment leaves behind

    At Mahanoy Area, the facts are now part of the school year. Two seniors stood as finalists for homecoming king. One was crowned. He gave the sash and crown to the other. Both names will stay attached to the story, Ramirez for what he gave up and Kuhla for the day he had waited years to reach.

    Homecoming titles pass quickly. Classes graduate and new finalists take the floor the next fall. What lasts in a school is often smaller and more concrete. Students who sat in that gym on Oct 16 saw a classmate treat a title as less important than the person standing next to him. Kindergartners in the crowd saw it too, in a districtwide rally that put the youngest and oldest students in the same room for the same moment.

    Ramirez put it in the shortest form when he spoke afterward. He did what any friend would have done. On Friday in Mahanoy, the whole gym watched a friend do exactly that.
